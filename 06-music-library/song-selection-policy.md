# Song Selection Policy

**Owner:** Song Selector + Director  

## Purpose

Curate a small, truthful, singable repertoire that forms this congregation over time. Selection serves corporate worship rather than platform novelty, personal taste, or entertainment.

## Policy (must)

### 1. Annual repertoire ceiling

- Use no more than **50 distinct congregational song titles in any rolling 12-month period**.
- Every full song the congregation is invited to sing counts, including seasonal songs, hymns, choruses, and each distinct title in a medley.
- A reprise of the same title does not count again. Instrumental underscoring and brief spoken or chanted liturgical responses are not repertoire titles unless treated as songs.
- The Song Selector maintains the annual repertoire register. Before adding title 51, retire or defer another title; the Director approves the decision.

The ceiling is a maximum, not a target. A smaller repertoire known deeply is preferable to a larger one known shallowly.

### 2. Core song criteria

Every song in the worship set must pass **all** of these criteria:

1. **Biblically defensible theology.** Its central claims and individual lines must be faithful to Scripture in context. A song need not quote Scripture verbatim, but the church must not have to excuse, repair, or privately reinterpret what it sings.
2. **Corporate-worship intent.** It must be intended for the gathered church to **sing together**, not merely for a soloist or band to perform while the congregation listens. A congregational arrangement does not by itself turn a performance song into a corporate-worship song.
3. **Congregational ownership.** The gathered body must be able to say the words truthfully together. Personal testimony and first-person singular language may serve the church, but the song cannot depend on an experience or claim the congregation cannot honestly share.
4. **Congregational singability.** Ordinary, untrained voices must be able to carry its melody, rhythm, range, repetition, and phrasing in a congregation-friendly key without needing a concert vocalist.
5. **Clear Christian confession.** The song must name or unmistakably confess the God and gospel of the Christian faith rather than offer generic transcendence, romance, or sentiment that only becomes Christian through explanation.
6. **Pastoral usefulness.** The song must help the church adore, remember, confess, lament, give thanks, petition, encourage one another, or respond to the Word and sacraments. A true and singable song may still be declined when it does not serve this congregation's present formation.

Therefore, do not select secular songs or generic “love songs” for the worship set. Instrumental walk-in, event, or transition music outside the worship set is a separate pastoral/programming decision; it is not approved by this policy merely because it contains no lyrics.

### 3. The congregation does not sing in God's first-person voice

- Do not select songs in which the congregation sings **as though it were God speaking** (“I, the Lord, say…”).
- First-person human response—“I worship,” “we believe,” “Lord, I need you”—is permitted, though the overall diet should favor language the gathered body can own together.
- Direct words of God may be read as Scripture with the speaker clearly identified. That is different from placing the congregation in God's role through song.

### 4. Week-to-week continuity

- Normally carry **at least one song from the immediately preceding week's service** into the next service.
- Prefer a meaningful repeat over a token repeat: use continuity to deepen congregational ownership, reinforce the preached or liturgical theme, or establish a new song.
- The Director may waive continuity for a major feast, special service, pastoral need, or a service with no suitable predecessor. Note the reason on the setlist worksheet.

## Selection tests (must pass)

| Test | Question |
|---|---|
| Biblically defensible theology | Are the lyrics faithful to Scripture in context, without a misleading claim or a line the church must repair? |
| Corporate-worship intent | Was the song made for the gathered church to sing together rather than merely hear performed? |
| Congregational ownership | Can the gathered body truthfully say these words together? |
| Congregational singability | Can ordinary voices carry the melody, rhythm, range, repetition, and phrasing in a congregation-friendly key? |
| Clear Christian confession | Does the song unmistakably confess the God and gospel of the Christian faith rather than generic transcendence or romance? |
| Pastoral usefulness | What work of Christian worship and formation does this song help this congregation do? |
| Repertoire fit | Does it add a needed function, or merely duplicate a stronger song already inside the 50-title ceiling? |
| Team stewardship | Can the scheduled team lead it reliably without making production the point? |

The Director holds final pastoral accountability. Theology-sensitive or disputed lyrics go to pastoral oversight before use.

## Repertoire-shaping defaults

These are defaults rather than absolute laws; exceptions should have a pastoral reason.

1. **One unfamiliar song at a time.** Do not ask the room to learn multiple songs in one service.
2. **Teach through repetition.** When introducing a song, plan its next uses before its first use. Aim for approximately three outings within six to eight weeks, including a repeat on the following week when suitable.
3. **Known songs carry new songs.** Build most services primarily from songs the congregation already owns.
4. **Prefer durable songs.** Favor texts and melodies likely to serve for years over songs selected because they are newly released or currently popular.
5. **Cover the whole Christian vocabulary.** Across the year, include adoration, creation, Christ's incarnation, cross, resurrection and reign, the Holy Spirit, confession, assurance, thanksgiving, lament, petition, mission, communion, baptism, and Christian hope as appropriate to the church's doctrine and practice.
6. **Balance pronouns and perspectives.** First-person singular human response has a place, but the annual diet should also teach the church to sing **we**, address **one another**, and tell what God has done.
7. **Let text govern treatment.** Key, tempo, dynamics, arrangement, and platform posture should clarify rather than contradict the words.
8. **Rest songs on purpose.** Familiarity is good; fatigue is real. Move overused titles to `Rest` and return them intentionally.
9. **Evaluate fruit, not applause.** Listen for congregational participation, intelligibility, pastoral usefulness, and durable recall—not only platform enthusiasm or streaming popularity.
10. **Check licensing and lyric accuracy.** Approval for theological and musical use does not bypass licensing, reporting, or proofreading obligations.

## New-song approval

Before a new title enters `Trial`:

- [ ] Confirm all six core song criteria in writing
- [ ] Complete a line-by-line lyric review, recording Scripture references and any concern or ambiguity
- [ ] Confirm it fits beneath the 50-title rolling ceiling
- [ ] Name what function it adds that the current repertoire lacks
- [ ] Choose a congregation-friendly key and teach strategy with the Worship Lead
- [ ] Schedule likely second and third outings
- [ ] Prepare accurate lyrics, chart, and licensing information
- [ ] Obtain Director approval; obtain pastoral review if theology-sensitive

## Review rhythm

### Per service

- Check for at least one previous-week carryover or record the exception.
- Identify whether any title is new, in trial, core, resting, or returning.
- Confirm every title appears in the repertoire register.

### Quarterly

- Count distinct titles used in the preceding 12 months.
- Review participation, excessive repetition, neglected themes, range, and seasonal needs.
- Move songs deliberately among `Trial`, `Core`, `Rest`, and `Archive`.

### Annually

- Review the full repertoire with pastoral oversight.
- Confirm doctrinal alignment and identify gaps in the church's sung theology.
- Publish the coming year's core list while preserving room under the ceiling for seasonal and genuinely needed additions.

## Exceptions

The Director may approve a one-service exception for a clear pastoral or liturgical reason, except that secular/generic love songs and first-person-God songs remain outside the worship-set policy. Record the exception and rationale. If exceptions recur, revise the policy openly rather than normalizing an unwritten rule.
